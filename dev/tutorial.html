Getting started

The pipeline has five stages, and each one is a function you can inspect.

1. Text to words

tokenize splits at full stops and keeps runs of letters, lowercased. Punctuation, digits and capitals are discarded — see Scaling up for how real models differ.

using CustomTokenizer
sentences = tokenize(TOY_TEXT)
sentences[1:2]
2-element Vector{Vector{String}}:
 ["the", "cat", "drinks", "milk"]
 ["the", "dog", "drinks", "water"]

2. Words to ids

build_vocab counts the words and sorts them most-frequent-first, so ids are stable and 1 is the commonest word.

vocab = build_vocab(sentences)
Vocabulary: 22 words, 62 tokens
   1  the        16
   2  a          6
   3  cat        4
   4  dog        3
   5  king       3
   6  queen      3
   7  castle     2
   8  chases     2
   9  crown      2
  10  drinks     2
  ⋮  (12 more)

62 words of text, 22 distinct. From here on the model sees only ids.

vocab["cat"], vocab[3], vocab.counts[vocab["cat"]]
(3, "cat", 4)

3. Two tables of numbers

Model allocates W (word vectors, kept) and C (context vectors, discarded). W starts small and random, C starts at exactly zero.

model = Model(vocab; dim = 8, window = 2, negative = 3)
Model: 8 numbers per word, 22 words
  window 2 · 3 negative samples per pair
  |W| largest entry 0.0624, |C| largest entry 0.0000

round.(wordvec(model, "cat"); digits = 4)
8-element Vector{Float64}:
  0.0301
  0.0467
  0.0174
  0.0148
 -0.0089
  0.0001
 -0.0098
 -0.0267

Every guess is 50/50 before training, because C is zero:

probability(model, "cat", "drinks")
0.5

4. Words to training pairs

corpus_pairs lists every (center, context) pair within the window. Pairs never cross a sentence boundary.

pairs = corpus_pairs(sentences, vocab; window = 2)
length(pairs)
176

cooccurrence is the same information as a table, and it is all the model will ever be told:

N = cooccurrence(sentences, vocab; window = 2)
sum(N), N == N'
(176, true)

King and queen have identical rows, which is why their vectors end up identical:

ik, iq = vocab["king"], vocab["queen"]
[(vocab[j], N[ik, j], N[iq, j]) for j in 1:length(vocab) if N[ik, j] + N[iq, j] > 0]
6-element Vector{Tuple{String, Int64, Int64}}:
 ("the", 4, 4)
 ("a", 1, 1)
 ("in", 1, 1)
 ("lives", 1, 1)
 ("rules", 1, 1)
 ("wears", 1, 1)

5. Training

train! runs update_pair! over every pair, reshuffling each epoch and drawing fresh negatives each time.

trainlog = train!(model, pairs; epochs = 300, snapshots = [0, 10, 300])
trainlog.losses[[1, 10, 100, 300]]
4-element Vector{Float64}:
 2.7723341751755513
 2.1302390063043197
 1.3034365833730381
 1.2887732619545487

The loss starts at 4ln2 = 2.77, the cost of guessing 50/50 on four questions, and comes down.

What came out

nearest_neighbours(model, "cat", 3)
3-element Vector{Tuple{String, Float64}}:
 ("dog", 0.7424391874164696)
 ("mouse", 0.717203446460063)
 ("milk", 0.6429088282119402)
[(a, b, round(similarity(model, a, b); digits = 2)) for (a, b) in
 (("king", "queen"), ("cat", "dog"), ("milk", "water"), ("cat", "king"))]
4-element Vector{Tuple{String, String, Float64}}:
 ("king", "queen", 1.0)
 ("cat", "dog", 0.74)
 ("milk", "water", 0.78)
 ("cat", "king", 0.18)

Nobody told the model that cats and dogs are animals. It found the grouping from the co-occurrence table alone.

Checking the result against the counts

Training settles where pulls and pushes cancel, at N / (N + E). target_probability computes that from the counts, and calibration compares the whole trained model against it:

probs = sampling_probabilities(UniformNegatives(), length(vocab))
target_probability(N, row_totals(N), probs, vocab["cat"], vocab["drinks"], 3)
0.4
calibration(model, N, probs)
(correlation = 0.9919336642427634, mean_absolute_error = 0.014133933342484289, n = 462)

A correlation above 0.99 is the sense in which word2vec "is" a factorisation of the co-occurrence table.
